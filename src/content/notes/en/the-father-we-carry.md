## Why Has China's Internet Industry Stopped Talking About Flat Management?

In 2006, a year after its founding, Google China moved into a new office building in Beijing's Tsinghua Science Park. In the press reports and employee blog posts of the time, it read like a utopia airlifted from the future: meeting rooms named after mythical realms from *Journey to the West*—the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, the Dragon Palace of the Eastern Sea, the Flaming Mountains—printers christened after Chinese spirits like Wuliangye and Zhuyeqing, open kitchens stocked with free drinks, fruit, and snacks, and even load-bearing columns and sofas painted in bright, cheerful colors.

What stirred young people even more than the decor was the alternative model of human relations it seemed to demonstrate. It was the era when China's internet industry was first looking up toward Silicon Valley, and a fascination with new technology naturally extended to imagining a new social order. Set against the traditional state sector—with its administrative ranks, strict seniority, and endless chains of approval—offices with slides, first-name terms, and young engineers who had a voice in company affairs felt like a gentle enlightenment. Domestic tech companies quickly followed suit. Tencent adopted English names; Alibaba popularized martial-arts nicknames. The goal was to use a moniker stripped of rank to usher the heavy, deferential title of "Director So-and-So" out of the office. As an article in Tencent Cloud's developer community once explained, English names were meant to soften hierarchy and lift the psychological weight of status.

Looking back twenty years later, there was an almost naive optimism to that faith in "flatness." People believed then that the arrogance of power lived mainly in old-fashioned titles and walled-off corner offices; tear down the partitions, put on T-shirts, and call the boss "Pony" or "Xiaoyaozi," and the suffocating conventions of the old workplace would naturally exit along with the titles.

Yet the disenchantment of form lasted only a brief honeymoon. What lay in the bones soon put on a new wardrobe and returned to center stage.

In 2017, Lu Qi delivered a keynote on artificial intelligence at the iQiyi World Conference. When the giant screen behind him switched to a slide illustrating the "Fourth Industrial Revolution," alongside the great waves of technological history appeared the portrait of Baidu's founder, Robin Li. A history belonging to all humanity had grown seamlessly onto the face of the company boss. After the slide was ridiculed online, Baidu hurried to explain that a test version had been projected by mistake. What lingered, however, was not the clerical error itself, but how effortlessly a familiar, timeworn posture had surfaced on a stage dedicated to rationality and the future.

It was no isolated slip. At some point, the most avidly shared posts on tech-company forums stopped being about how to write elegant code and became guides on how to catch the boss's cues in a group chat, how to wrap a dissenting view as a humble footnote to a superior's foresight, and how to slip flattery unobtrusively into weekly reports. When screenshots of an internal work group from a major tech ecosystem circulated online—showing subordinates lining up to shower their superior with quasi-devotional praise—netizens mocked the shameless sycophancy in public while quietly saving the screenshots in private as a textbook for workplace survival.

An industry that once promised to free people from studying status ended up using the most advanced collaboration software to train status-reading into a round-the-clock muscle memory. Today, few speak of flat management with the old fervor. English names and martial-arts nicknames remain printed on badges, and the companies still charge ahead commercially, yet the air inside the office has settled back into the heavy stillness of a one-man court.

Why, after importing the most modern organizational shells, did we still grow the old patterns of dependence and submission inside them? If we look away from the office tower and trace a person's life further back, we find that long before walking into any company, most of us had already undergone a twenty-year apprenticeship in power.

## How the Father Takes Hold Within Us

There is an unforgettable scene in the television drama [*My Chief and My Regiment*](https://m.tvmao.com/drama/blNPLQ%3D%3D/episode/8-27): Meng Fanliao, a sharp-tongued soldier who has survived the crucible of war, returns home and comes face-to-face with his father. However much absurdity and resentment fill his chest, his knees bend first. Before anything else can happen, he must kneel on the ground and receive his father's lecture.

Traditional Chinese family narratives return again and again to this composition: the elder sits upright in the hall, the younger kneels below, and reason and reprimand flow exclusively from top to bottom. In this order, right and wrong are secondary; position comes first. A father holds the privilege of anger, fault-finding, and disappointment. A son, no matter how capable he has become in the outside world, must cross the threshold by first putting away his adult dignity, assuming the posture of a subordinate, and only then pleading his case.

If a stern father's lecture still carries visible coercion, another kind of kneeling—wrapped in deep affection—reveals even more clearly how durable this order is. In the film [*Forever Young*](https://www.cnhan.com/html/yule/20180208/719597.htm), Shen Guangyao, played by Wang Leehom, resolves to join the air force in wartime. His mother travels a great distance to dissuade him, and her first words require him to kneel and recite their ancestral precepts. Her tears are scalding. She does not ask her son for fame or glory; she asks only that he study, travel, marry someone he loves, and live out his days in peace. No one could doubt the depth of her maternal love. Yet precisely within that most moving tenderness, the grown son's conversation with his mother is still conducted on his knees.

Here lies the subtlest and most intractable knot of Chinese family life: supreme affection and supreme hierarchy can fuse without the slightest friction. Love does not dissolve rank; instead, it coats rank in a warm, solemn glow, making submission appear self-evident and turning resistance into a betrayal of kinship.

Nor does this relationship require physical proximity to hold its shape. When I was studying in Taiwan, a professor once told me about his upbringing. Raised under strict discipline, he had to wake at five every morning to read and write a daily journal reporting his studies to his family. Later, he went to Germany for graduate study. By my reckoning, he was already a scholar approaching thirty, yet an iron rule from home remained in force: every month he had to write his father a family letter, and it had to be written with a traditional brush in formal *kaishu*—regular script.

Those words—*brush and regular script*—have stayed with me for years. In late-twentieth-century Germany, a grown man trained in modern Western scholarship could not merely write home to share his life; both the instrument in his hand and the strokes on the page had to conform strictly to his father's command across oceans. Neither geographical distance nor modern education had retired his father's ruler.

Whether it is the bent knees on screen or the brush-written letters on a desk in Europe, both point to the same piercing question: In our culture, how old must a person grow before he can stand up in front of his parents as an adult entitled to his own decisions?

In many families, biological adulthood and financial independence do not automatically confer equal standing. Parents retain a perpetual right of review and a veto over their children's lives. A deeper transformation takes place inside the mind: once external rules have been repeated a thousand times, the elder sitting in the hall is gradually internalized as an inner yardstick. The father no longer needs to slap the table, or even open his mouth; before making any major choice, the child has already rehearsed the furrow of his brow, his sigh, and his thunderous displeasure.

This is what I call the "spiritual father"—the father we carry within us. The role need not be played by a biological father; a mother, an elder, or any family authority can occupy the seat. Its power is formidable because it rests on the moral bedrock of bloodline, family precepts, and the debt of upbringing, refusing to abdicate simply because the child has grown up.

Today, most families have long since abandoned the ritual of kneeling, and no one forces adult children to write home with an ink brush. Yet a relaxation of etiquette is not a transfer of decision-making power. In many families where rural ties remain tight and seniority remains sacred, what major an adult chooses, what organization they join, whom they marry, and where they live must still pass parental inspection.

When a person has breathed this air for twenty years and steps into society trained in reading faces, their sense of smell for power is extraordinarily acute. They know all too well the half-second of silence before an authority figure erupts, and they know instinctively when to cut short an explanation and offer a posture of deference first. So when they sit in a company conference room across from a manager who treats a disagreement over work as an affront to personal authority, they need no orientation. They recognize at once the conversation from their childhood dinner table: the merits of the issue no longer matter; the urgent task is to drop to one's knees figuratively and account for one's attitude.

## How the Strict Father Is Inherited

If this order of authority is so suffocating, why doesn't the younger generation bury it once they grow up and take power themselves? Why, instead, do they so often reproduce it intact?

A memory from my primary school classroom offers a clue. When I was a child, the class monitor was allowed to hit people.

The teacher handed the wooden pointer—the symbol of disciplinary power—to the monitor and permitted him to strike classmates judged to be out of line. The monitor was ten years old, no different from anyone else, except that he held a mandate from a higher authority. What was remarkable was that the teacher who handed over the pointer, the monitor who swung it, and the classmates who held out their palms to be struck all treated the arrangement as the most natural thing in the world.

Looking back at that classroom years later, what chills me most is not the crisp snap of the pointer, but the seeds that miniature lesson in power planted in different children's minds. The child holding the pointer tasted early the intoxication of exercising delegated power over fearful peers. And what did the children being struck learn through their pain?

Some, to be sure, came away with a lifelong revulsion toward privilege and bullying. Yet many others drew a very different philosophy of survival: *The reason I am being hit, humiliated, and silenced today is not that hitting people is wrong in itself, but simply that I am not yet strong enough or high enough in rank. If I clench my teeth and conform to the rules harder than anyone else, one day the pointer will be in my hand.*

That hope—"one day I can take his place"—possesses astonishing resilience. It can sustain a person through years of harsh exploitation and humiliation in a subordinate position, entering every grievance into a ledger for the future, waiting for the day the tables turn to collect with interest. By the time he finally claws his way up—becoming a parent, a supervisor, a figure others must look up to—the bitterness he once swallowed does not make him compassionate toward the weak. Instead, it furnishes the most self-righteous justification when he swings the pointer himself: *"I went through the exact same thing."*

What he hated was never the hierarchical order itself; what he hated was only that he once occupied its lowest rung. As for whether the strong ought to treat the weak that way, that question was set aside the moment he decided to trade endurance for eventual dominance.

Here is the secret mechanism by which the "strict father" is passed down from generation to generation: a son who loathes his father's tyranny can easily grow into an equally tyrannical father; a subordinate who grits his teeth at a manager's humiliation can demand that his own staff swallow the same humiliation once he takes charge. So long as victims place their ultimate redemption in becoming perpetrators, the wheel of power never truly stops turning.

Of course, as social norms evolve and overt corporal punishment or verbal abuse becomes harder to justify in public, the visible pointer adapts by turning invisible. In the modern workplace, it morphs into what people in China now call "soft PUA"—subtle psychological manipulation: repeatedly negating your worth until you are convinced you have no future except under a particular boss's wing; doling out approval like bait and snatching it back so you remain trapped in anxious self-proving; and, the moment you think of leaving, spreading out every past favor as an unpayable debt of gratitude. The wooden pointer is gone, but the strict father who monopolizes the measure of your worth—keeping you docile through self-doubt—still sits firmly in his chair.

## When Obedience Is Called Filial Piety and Deference Becomes a Duty

If power operated solely through fear and coercion, a person could still preserve an enclave of rebellion in the heart. What makes it truly disarming is when power puts on the garments of morality and warmth, defining obedience as "filial piety" and labeling deference as "knowing one's place."

Disputes over seats on public buses offer a small slice of what happens when this ethic of seniority spills beyond the home. When an older passenger demands a seat from a younger commuter and is refused, the focus of the argument almost always pivots within seconds—away from who is physically unwell or needs the seat more, and straight into a moral tribunal over whether the young person "knows the rules" or "was raised properly." So long as *"I am older than you"* serves as a trump card, two strangers who are modern citizens on equal footing are instantly forced into a makeshift clan hierarchy.

If that holds among strangers, once we return to the flesh-and-blood interior of the family, the ethic carries a suffocating moral gravity. The perennial drama of *cuihun*—pressuring adult children to marry—is its classic battlefield.

A young adult who has long since come of age and makes an independent living in the city remains, in the eyes of parents, an unfinished, defective project simply for being unmarried. Blind dates must be arranged in rapid succession, the progress of every meeting must be interrogated in detail, and even why a previous relationship ended without marriage requires an explanation that satisfies the elders.

In this tug-of-war, the adult child's misery is obvious: private emotional territory is repeatedly invaded, and intimacy—which naturally takes time and trial to discover—is turned into an exam paper that must be handed in before a deadline. Heavier still is the emotional collective punishment: turning down a match means spending twice the energy afterward consoling disappointed parents, while confessing that one has no desire to marry at all is tantamount to detonating a bomb at a quiet family dinner.

Yet if we see only parental high-handedness, we underestimate the complexity of the tragedy. In truth, parents who press their children to marry are carrying an invisible mountain of their own. Under China's traditional intergenerational contract, parental responsibility has neither boundaries nor an expiration date. So long as a child remains unmarried, parents feel their mission in life is incomplete; a casual inquiry from a relative or neighbor can easily sting them with guilt and anxiety that they have "failed to settle their child's life properly." In their understanding, letting go is not respect—it is dereliction of duty. Relentless prodding is precisely the proof that they love their child with all their might and are trying to secure their child's future.

And so two generations fall into a deadlock in which each holds the other prisoner: parents cannot put down the infinite responsibility of managing their child's life, and children cannot shake off the infinite obligation of using their life choices to soothe their parents' anxieties. A child's temporary concession may buy a few days of peace, yet it invisibly reconfirms the parents' right to intervene; the more anxiously parents press forward, the less the child dares to reveal genuine hesitation or vulnerability, retreating behind perfunctory compliance and evasion.

Every argument eventually crashes into that impregnable wall of sighs: *"Isn't everything we do for your own good?"*

The phrase strikes people speechless because it uses the purity of motive to obscure the violation of boundaries. The goodwill may be a hundred percent real, yet the core question—*Whose life is this, and who ought to make the decision?*—is tenderly erased. When filial piety is narrowed into an outcome audit measured by whether parents feel at ease, even an adult who cares for their parents attentively and loves them deeply can be branded "selfish" and "inconsiderate" simply for living according to their own wishes. Love and obedience are lashed together on the same scale; to reclaim the right to decide your own life, you must first bear the cross of having betrayed the debt of your upbringing.

Once you understand this emotional ledger at home, the strange phenomena of Chinese corporate life cease to look strange at all. When an employee wants to resign for a new opportunity and a manager asks in pained reproach, *"After all the years the company has cultivated you, how can you justify this?"*; when a subordinate questions an unreasonable assignment and a superior admonishes earnestly, *"Why else would I pile this weight on you if not for your own future?"*—this is hardly a contractual dialogue in a modern commercial society. It is a workplace remake of *"We're doing this for your own good"* from the family dinner table. For someone steeped since childhood in feelings of indebtedness and the ethics of repayment, hearing that script triggers the string of guilt long before they have had a chance to ask what they actually want.

## How Family Authority Enters Company Management

Precisely because this psychological soil runs so deep, many Chinese enterprises inadvertently run modern corporations as enlarged clans.

In 2024, internal remarks by JD.com founder Richard Liu about his "brothers" once again stirred public debate. A founder who had countless times thumped his chest in public, calling frontline workers his "brothers" and promising them generous protection, drew a sharp new line when faced with slowing performance and organizational slackness: anyone who performed poorly and never fought hard was no longer his brother ([related report](https://www.36kr.com/p/2796453153092481)).

What is most revealing about the episode is not the commercial logic of weeding out low performers, but how the word "brother"—heavy with the overtones of sworn brotherhood and clan kinship—was used to wrap an employment relationship. Under a pure modern contract, job performance corresponds to pay and position. Once the narrative of "brotherhood" is introduced, however, performance evaluation transforms into a moral judgment of kinship and belonging. Work yourself to the bone, and you earn not just a salary, but the favor and shelter of the "elder brother"; fail to keep pace, and you lose not just a job, but your place on the family roll. As for who qualifies as a brother and when the definition changes, the power of interpretation remains monopolized by the patriarch who controls the resources.

Management scholars studying Chinese organizations have long analyzed this pattern under the rubric of "paternalistic leadership": authoritarianism and benevolence are never mutually exclusive opposites, but two sides of the same coin. The leader monopolizes decisions and demands absolute obedience like a stern father, while extending solicitude and protection to compliant subordinates like a caring parent. An [empirical study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1048984314000034) of supervisors and employees in Chinese firms clearly demonstrated a significant negative correlation between authoritarian leadership behaviors and employee voice. Under a paternalistic roof, in other words, the warmth of being looked after and the fear of speaking up can easily coexist in the same employee's workday.

Within this logic, the desire for control is effortlessly beautified as a sense of responsibility. Just as parents are convinced their child will go astray without their supervision, a paternalistic leader sincerely believes that only by personally reviewing every detail is he fulfilling his duty to the company and his people. And the more subordinates ask for instructions out of awe, the more they feed the leader's psychological satisfaction in being needed and obeyed. Over time, delegating power comes to feel like a dangerous, unsettling act.

This explains why Western-style flat management so often mutates when transplanted into this soil. A founder may be open-minded enough to let the whole company call him by his English name, yet unable to tolerate a young engineer publicly pointing out a strategic blunder in a company-wide group chat. Cutting out middle management and shortening reporting lines is meant to empower the front line; in a culture lacking checks on power, however, removing the middle layers often concentrates life-and-death authority even more directly and nakedly in the hands of a single supreme ruler. The organizational chart does indeed become flatter, while personal autocracy grows steeper than ever.

Perceptive employees need only a few days to read the unwritten rules behind the tissue paper: the slogan on the wall says *"Encourage Independent Thinking,"* but year-end promotions go consistently to those who know best how to put the boss's mind at ease; the meeting agenda calls for *"Speaking Freely,"* but anyone who actually contradicts a superior in the room must spend ten times the effort afterward privately explaining their "attitude problem." The instinct for self-preservation quickly takes over, converting precious sharpness and creativity into safe, unimpeachable silence.

And so the grand experiment of flat management is quietly rewritten through a thousand small, unremarked moments: a new hire learns to call the boss by an English name on day one, and six months later learns when to shut up as soon as the boss opens his mouth.

## What Taiwan Can Teach Us

Is a society rooted in East Asian culture—one that prizes family and human bonds—doomed to circle endlessly inside a warm-hearted autocracy? Years ago, a semester as an exchange student in Taiwan pushed open an unexpected window for me.

While studying there, I was struck by how casually my Taiwanese classmates brought their boyfriends or girlfriends home to have dinner with their parents. Some young people were still exploring in their relationships and changed partners fairly often; a month or two later, the face brought to the family dinner table might already be someone new. Yet their parents still warmly set out an extra bowl and pair of chopsticks, chatting about everyday things without putting on an inquisitorial air, let alone stepping in to make the decision for their child.

That ordinary detail shook me at the time. In the environment where I grew up, "bringing a partner home to meet the parents" was an almost quasi-judicial procedure—a sign that a romance had reached the stage of family inspection, where the partner's family background, job, temperament, and prospects were laid on the table for the parents to deliver a final verdict on whether the match was suitable and allowed to continue. Precisely because the stakes were so high, young people rarely breathed a word of a relationship to their parents until they were nearly certain of the outcome.

Those casual dinners at my Taiwanese classmates' homes showed me a completely different possibility: romance could flow naturally through everyday family life. Parents knew, participated, and were present, yet their presence did not mean the child had to surrender the right to judge the relationship. Intimacy and boundaries, it turned out, could exist side by side.

What moved me even more was how they talked about politics at the dinner table. In many Taiwanese families I encountered, the older generation were staunch supporters of the Kuomintang, while the younger generation leaned toward the Democratic Progressive Party or newer parties. Sitting around the same table, two generations would often argue over a public issue until their faces turned red. Yet an argument remained an argument: elders did not slam the table and storm out accusing their children of filial rebellion simply for disagreeing, nor did young people have to surrender their ballot or political convictions as proof of filial devotion.

Taiwan serves as such an illuminating mirror precisely because it is not an alien civilization built on rugged individualism across the ocean, but a Chinese society sharing the same roots and steeped in the same Confucian family ethics. As my Taiwanese professor—who still had to write his father brush-written letters in regular script at nearly thirty—attested in his own life, Taiwan too walked out of a stern patriarchal tradition step by step.

Yet as social modernization progressed and civic consciousness took root, traditional family relations there completed a crucial separation—disentangling *love and care* from *control and possession*. Parents still love their children deeply and still cherish family reunions, but they have gradually learned to stop before an invisible boundary, acknowledging that the child sitting across from them is an independent human being with the right to make mistakes and the freedom to hold different views.

For me, "equality"—an otherwise abstract modern political word—first took on a warm, tangible shape at those ordinary family dinners: parents did not have to endorse every choice their child made, and a child did not have to win parental approval before beginning their own life. Disagreements remained on the table, and dinner could still be eaten together in good spirits.

## Family Culture Is a Microscope for Social Power

Looking back at that old blog post from twenty years ago when Google China moved into Tsinghua Science Park, there is a small detail tucked inside that is even more telling than the colorful sofas and free snacks: some employees publicly complained that they had not been consulted when the meeting rooms were named, and the colleagues in charge of administration, far from feeling offended, readily promised to put the naming rights to an all-hands vote next time.

Reading that line today, I finally understand what made that golden age of "flatness" so captivating. Its core appeal was never the abolition of the title "Director." It was that a junior, ordinary employee could object to a rule openly, confident that doing so would not invite retaliation from above—and that those holding power could treat a subordinate's complaint as normal feedback rather than a challenge to their personal authority.

That sense of security, built on equal personhood, cannot be conjured out of thin air by an employee handbook.

A person who grew up watching the teacher hand the pointer to the class monitor and a person who grew up accustomed to debating elders as equals may walk into the same flat-management tech giant on the same morning, yet they are looking at two entirely different worlds. For the latter, disagreement in a discussion is as natural as breathing. For the former, a leader's willingness to step down from the dais and listen is already an act of "open-mindedness" and "grace" that calls for gratitude—and because it is grace, the moment a sensitive nerve is touched, that license to speak freely can be revoked at any time.

This is how the family quietly shapes the texture of society. It never drafts corporate management bylaws directly, yet when a person is just learning to speak and make sense of the world, it writes a foundational code of power into the unconscious through daily glances, tones of voice, rewards, punishments, and tears: whose voice naturally carries more weight, whose anger demands unconditional capitulation, and which parts of one's own life must be surrendered in exchange for the shelter of love. When schools and workplaces receive these already formed souls, they merely reinforce that childhood code round after round using grades, ranks, and KPIs.

So when we lament why workplaces keep sliding back into one-man rule, or why society struggles to bring forth genuine equality and democracy, our gaze cannot linger only on grand institutional designs or fashionable management slogans. We have to turn the microscope onto the family routines we take most for granted. A person can bitterly resent a boss's arbitrariness in a work chat during the day, and then come home at night and fly into a rage because their child refuses to follow the path arranged for them. Whether our criticism of arbitrary power springs from a belief in equality or merely from frustration at not yet holding the whip ourselves is tested most nakedly the moment we become parents, elders, or managers.

Those ordinary dinners where my Taiwanese classmates brought their partners home have stayed with me because they proved that change is not an impossible luxury. Taking off the yoke of the "strict father" does not cause the family to fall apart; parents remain parents, care and advice can continue, and the child finally gains an open expanse of life that requires no prior approval.

More than twenty years ago, we eagerly gave everyone in the office English names and nicknames, believing that changing how we addressed one another would usher in an equal new world. More than two decades later, after the tide of surface forms has receded, perhaps it is time to turn around and look at the familiar dinner table at home: Can the child who has long since grown up calmly state a decision about their own life today, without first having to ask their parents for forgiveness before opening their mouth?
